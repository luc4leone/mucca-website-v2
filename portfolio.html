I simplify complex software

Dozens of parameters, expert users, hardware that pushes back. The job is deciding what goes on the one screen that matters — and leaving everything else out of the way.

Next2 — automatic fabric cutter

Morgan Tecnica · UX/UI + physical console · Windows touch desktop · 2020

HMI & embedded

Product
Cut room software for an automatic fabric cutter, plus the physical console that drives the cutting head. Used by factory operators, most of them not at ease with a PC.
Complexity
Between 50 and 100 parameters, a long multi-step workflow, hardware constraints inherited from the previous machine. Everything had to be redesigned from scratch without breaking what the plant already did.
Immersion
Six months of factory visits, watching operators on the old machine before drawing a single screen.
Made obvious
Cockpit logic: almost no navigation, no controls hidden in menus, labels in the operators' own jargon, targets sized for a working hand. The console separates left-hand and right-hand commands so the body remembers what the mind used to.
Result
In production. No numbers from the client, but the feedback was direct: far fewer errors on the console, "much more ergonomic".

Fox — automatic fabric spreader, two generations

Morgan Tecnica · UX/UI + physical console · Tablet touch · 2015–2020

HMI & embedded

Product
Spreader that unrolls fabric rolls into lays for cutting. Fox 2 is the mechanical and software update of Fox 1, not a rewrite.
Complexity
Manual mode ran on a switch plus a handle: modal, sequence-based, memorised. Errors were frequent enough that operators avoided it altogether. Data entry went through a virtual keyboard nobody wanted to use.
Immersion
Six months on the factory floor for the first generation. The spreading UI was then redesigned three times over five years, as the machine evolved.
Made obvious
The console works like a car seat control: one button per motor, laid out as a map of the machine, so the operator recognises instead of recalling. On screen, numbers are entered by finger, not by keyboard; roll change is three numbered steps.
Result
Both generations in production. The console was the turning point: fewer errors, and operators started trusting the manual commands again.

Ply1 — single-ply cutter

Morgan Tecnica · UX/UI · Windows, 24" touchscreen · 2021

HMI & embedded

Product
Single-ply cutter with linear motors, a new machine for the client. The interface had to feel as new as the mechanics.
Complexity
Dozens of nested pieces, cutting parameters, three tools, camera, conveyor — all live, all on one touch surface.
Immersion
Built on the Next2 work: same plant, same operators, same six months of observation behind it.
Made obvious
The cockpit idea taken to its limit: no navigation at all. One screen, the marker in the middle, everything else at the edges. Light background to tell it apart from the multi-ply line at a glance.
Result
In production.

Twist — fabric spreader

Morgan Tecnica · UX/UI · Touch panel · 2017

HMI & embedded

Product
Another spreader model: same job as Fox, different machine.
Complexity
A hard requirement: the PLC register number (DM) of every parameter had to stay visible for technicians. Usability for operators and traceability for technicians on the same screen.
Immersion
Same client, same factory floor as Fox: the second of three spreading UIs I designed for them.
Made obvious
Oversized arrows and values: one glance, one tap. Register numbers kept, but pushed under each control where only those who look for them see them.
Result
In production, fewer input errors.

Sock knitting machine

Lonati · UX/UI · Linux touch panel · 2014

HMI & embedded

Product
Control panel of an industrial sock knitting machine.
Complexity
The previous UI was windows and menus from the '80s. The whole interaction paradigm had to change, for operators who had used the old one for years, under neon lights that wash out any screen. Some operations are dangerous and must need both hands.
Immersion
Time on the machine and on the old software, mapping which of its screens were actually used. Dieter Rams' Braun layouts as the reference for aligning controls.
Made obvious
Far fewer screens. Production data live on the main view instead of buried in status bars. The sock's progress — leg, heel, sole — as a timeline you can read from across the room. Two-hand safety on the touchscreen itself, not on a button somewhere else. Every state of every control designed for glare.
Result
Presented at the trade fair, went into production.

Egro One Touch — self-service coffee machine

Rancilio Group · UX/UI · 7" touchscreen · 2014

HMI & embedded

Product
Coffee machine for hotels and bars: about 20 drinks, customisation, payment. Anyone, any language, no time to learn.
Complexity
Every drink has size, strength and extras; some combinations exist, some don't. Credit changes what you can order. All on a 7" screen.
Immersion
At least ten days at the plant next to the machine, running the flows on paper before touching pixels.
Made obvious
One decision per screen, no back button: a short linear flow you cannot get lost in. The "Make drink" button repeats the cup size, so nobody puts the wrong cup under the spout.
Result
Shipped. First machines installed in June 2015 at the Hyatt McCormick Place in Chicago.

Egro — second machine, larger screen

Rancilio Group · UX/UI · Touch · 2015

HMI & embedded

Product
The next machine, one year later, with a bigger display and full drink customisation.
Complexity
More ingredients to adjust — foam, milk, coffee, size — and a customer who has to understand what they are changing.
Immersion
Second year with the same client and the same machines.
Made obvious
The cup is the interface: it fills with the actual ingredients as you adjust them. Not decoration — it shows what you are about to drink.
Result
In production.
