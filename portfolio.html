I simplify complex software

Dozens of parameters, expert users, hardware that pushes back. The job is deciding what goes on the one screen that matters — and leaving everything else out of the way.

Next2 — automatic fabric cutter

Morgan Tecnica · UX/UI + physical console · Windows touch desktop · 2020

Cockpit logic: almost no navigation, and far fewer errors on the console.

4 images — scroll or use ← →

Product
Cut room software for an automatic fabric cutter, plus the physical console that drives the cutting head. Used by factory operators, most of them not at ease with a PC.
Complexity
Between 50 and 100 parameters, a long multi-step workflow, hardware constraints inherited from the previous machine. Everything had to be redesigned from scratch without breaking what the plant already did.
Immersion
Six months of factory visits, watching operators on the old machine before drawing a single screen.
Made obvious
Cockpit logic: almost no navigation, no controls hidden in menus, labels in the operators' own jargon, targets sized for a working hand. The console separates left-hand and right-hand commands so the body remembers what the mind used to.
Result
In production. No numbers from the client, but the feedback was direct: far fewer errors on the console, "much more ergonomic".

Fox — automatic fabric spreader, two generations

Morgan Tecnica · UX/UI + physical console · Tablet touch · 2015–2020

A console mapped on the machine body, the way a car seat control is.

10 images — scroll or use ← →

Product
Spreader that unrolls fabric rolls into lays for cutting. Fox 2 is the mechanical and software update of Fox 1, not a rewrite.
Complexity
Manual mode ran on a switch plus a handle: modal, sequence-based, memorised. Errors were frequent enough that operators avoided it altogether. Data entry went through a virtual keyboard nobody wanted to use.
Immersion
Six months on the factory floor for the first generation. The spreading UI was then redesigned three times over five years, as the machine evolved.
Made obvious
The console works like a car seat control: one button per motor, laid out as a map of the machine, so the operator recognises instead of recalling. On screen, numbers are entered by finger, not by keyboard; roll change is three numbered steps.
Result
Both generations in production. The console was the turning point: fewer errors, and operators started trusting the manual commands again.

Sock knitting machine

Lonati · UX/UI · Linux touch panel · 2014

Far fewer screens, and the sock's progress readable across the room.

5 images — scroll or use ← →

Product
Control panel of an industrial sock knitting machine.
Complexity
The previous UI was windows and menus from the '80s. The whole interaction paradigm had to change, for operators who had used the old one for years, under neon lights that wash out any screen. Some operations are dangerous and must need both hands.
Immersion
Time on the machine and on the old software, mapping which of its screens were actually used. Dieter Rams' Braun layouts as the reference for aligning controls.
Made obvious
Far fewer screens. Production data live on the main view instead of buried in status bars. The sock's progress — leg, heel, sole — as a timeline you can read from across the room. Two-hand safety on the touchscreen itself, not on a button somewhere else. Every state of every control designed for glare.
Result
Presented at the trade fair, went into production.

Mediaddress — media relations platform

Mediaddress · UX/UI · Web app · 2015–2016, redesigned 2025–2026

Nine tasks out of nine completed, against five give-ups on the old design.

4 images — scroll or use ← →

Product
The largest Italian media relations platform. Press offices search for journalists, build lists, and send releases to them.
Complexity
Not the number of filters. The hard part is that what people describe in interviews often cannot be asked of the database at all, because the metadata is not there. And sending to a list is its own long flow of options.
Immersion
Countless interviews and usability tests. I designed this software twice, ten years apart.
Made obvious
Filters shaped around what people are trying to achieve, not around the database schema. Results as journalist cards you can read, instead of rows in a table.
Result
A randomised test, two groups of three first-time users, three tasks each. On the new design all nine tasks were completed (averages 27", 2'58", 2'34"). On the old one, five of the nine ended with the user giving up.

I make an ecommerce sell

A shop is a funnel with a product at the end of it. Most of the money is lost in the steps nobody looks at.

Glowal — made-to-measure skincare

Glowal, now Beautytune · UX/UI and conversion · Mobile-first ecommerce

Conversion from 0.30% to 2%. From €2,000 to €200,000 in a year.

5 images — scroll or use ← →

Product
A skincare startup that formulates a cream for your face. A quiz and a skin reading decide the formula; the products are made in three days.
Complexity
The funnel. In the original version you took the quiz, waited for a swab to arrive by post, sent back a photo of it, and only then could you buy. It did not work.
Immersion
I studied the product and how it is customised, the whole flow, and the numbers from the first six months — the site was already live when I arrived.
Made obvious
The quiz and the skin reading moved inside the site: nothing has to arrive in the post before you can buy. The result screen shows what was found, zone by zone, so the formula is explained rather than asserted.
Result
€2,000 in the first six months on the original site. €200,000 in the twelve months after the redesign. Ecommerce conversion rate from 0.30% to 2%.

Egro One Touch — self-service coffee machine

Rancilio Group · UX/UI · 7" touchscreen · 2014

One decision per screen, no back button. First installed in Chicago, 2015.

6 images — scroll or use ← →

Product
Coffee machine for hotels and bars: about 20 drinks, customisation, payment. Anyone, any language, no time to learn.
Complexity
Every drink has size, strength and extras; some combinations exist, some don't. Credit changes what you can order. All on a 7" screen.
Immersion
At least ten days at the plant next to the machine, running the flows on paper before touching pixels.
Made obvious

Result
Shipped. First machines installed in June 2015 at the Hyatt McCormick Place in Chicago.

I find what's wrong with interfaces

Before redesigning anything, find out what is actually broken — and prove it, rather than assert it.

Poinx — couponing ecommerce

Poinx · Product & UX/UI · 2014

Labels from card sorting, a cart, and guest checkout — new in Italy then.

2 images — scroll or use ← →

Product
Couponing ecommerce: deals on restaurants, treatments, courses, trips.
Complexity
Information architecture. People could not find the deal they wanted, because deals were not grouped the way people expect. On top of that: filters, an L-shaped navigation, and no cart at all — you could not buy two coupons together.
Immersion
A lot of card sorting, to find out which semantic group people expected a given deal to live in. Then the Baymard Institute checkout guidelines for the purchase flow.
Made obvious
Groups and labels taken from the card sorting, not from the company's internal categories — the labels got a disproportionate share of my attention. A cart. And guest checkout, new for the Italian market at the time.
Result
The team considered guest checkout a success, though I have no number for it. The site no longer exists.
